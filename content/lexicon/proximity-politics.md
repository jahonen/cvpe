**Proximity Politics** *(n.)* — The emerging EU doctrine by which border management authority devolves to frontline states: de facto first, de jure later.

The pattern is consistent. A pressure event occurs at an external border — [Ceuta in July 2026](/essays/ceuta-first-evergray-battle/) being the founding case study. The frontline state acts unilaterally, outside or beyond the common framework, because the common framework moves at treaty speed and the pressure moves at evergray speed. Brussels objects, then acquiesces, then retroactively frames the unilateral action as consistent with European values and existing law. The exception hardens into precedent. The precedent hardens into doctrine. No treaty is amended; the treaty simply stops describing reality.

Proximity politics is therefore not a policy anyone announced. It is a doctrine being assembled by accumulation, visible only if you track the sequence of exceptions. Authority is migrating to whoever is closest to the pressure point, because proximity confers both the capability and the political licence to act — and because the centre has repeatedly demonstrated it will ratify after the fact rather than enforce before it.

## Development

Coined 31 July 2026 in the analysis of the Ceuta events, where a manufactured migration surge — a textbook [evergray](/lexicon/evergray-wars) instrument — was met with measures that had no basis in the common asylum framework and were nonetheless absorbed into it within weeks. The term generalises from migration to the broader pattern: energy interconnects, subsea repair jurisdiction, and airspace closures are following the same de-facto-first path.

## Implications

Proximity politics can be read two ways, and [Ahonen's Razor](/lexicon/ahonens-razor) requires keeping them separate. As a *problem*: it erodes the legal order the Union claims as its foundation. As a *solution*: it may be the only mechanism by which a 27-member consensus system can operate at evergray tempo. The audit question this site will keep asking: is the de jure ever actually catching up, or is "de facto first, de jure later" quietly becoming "de facto only"?
