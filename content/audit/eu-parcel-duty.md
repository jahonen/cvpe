On 1 July 2026, the EU scrapped its €150 duty-free threshold for low-value parcels and replaced it with a flat €3 duty per tariff classification within a shipment — a temporary measure running to 2028, ahead of a full customs overhaul. The target was explicit: low-value parcel imports had grown from 1.4 billion EU-wide in 2022 to 5.8 billion in 2025, over 90% from China, built almost entirely on the direct-shipping model Shein, Temu, and AliExpress developed specifically to exploit the exemption.

Pre-implementation opposition was sharper than ordinary industry grumbling, and it came partly from inside the institution passing the law. The [European Parliament's own trade committee](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/committees/en/inta/home) chair, Bernd Lange, conceded before implementation that enforcement "is barely manageable, and normal checks are almost impossible." Athens Commercial Association vice president Makis Savvidis predicted customs staff would simply wave shipments through at the flat rate regardless of actual contents, given the sheer volume involved. Logistics analyst Derek Lossing forecast a 10–35% fall in air-shipped e-commerce volume in the weeks after implementation, but flagged the structural difference from the US precedent: when the US closed its own de minimis exemption in 2025, Temu and Shein redirected growth investment into Europe — this time, with the EU closing the same gap, there is no comparably open major market left to redirect into.

The first real result, published today, is dramatic and comes from official government data, not analyst estimate. [Finnish Customs (Tulli)](https://tulli.fi/en/frontpage) statistics, reported by [Yle](https://yle.fi/), show the volume of low-value shipments into Finland fell more than 66% month-on-month and more than 76% year-on-year in July — the first full month of the duty. Finland received nearly 55 million sub-€150 shipments in 2025, a 69% increase on the prior year, over 98% of them from China. [Kaupan liitto](https://kauppa.fi/) (the Finnish Commerce Federation) CEO Kari Luoto called the drop proof that the EU can act quickly and effectively when it chooses to — a notably strong endorsement from an industry body, and one worth weighing seriously rather than dismissing as a trade group taking a victory lap. A separate Kaupan liitto survey found Finnish web traffic to Chinese e-commerce platforms also fell across 2026, a demand-side signal independent of the customs count.

What keeps this at PARTIAL rather than PASS: three real, still-open questions, not hedging for its own sake.

First, this is one member state's data. EU-wide, the Commission's own mandated monthly monitoring of import diversion does not begin until 1 October 2026 — there is no confirmed bloc-wide figure yet, and Finland may not be representative.

Second, a collapse in the parcel count crossing this specific threshold is not the same thing as a collapse in Chinese platforms' actual EU sales. Both Temu and Shein spent the eight months between the November 2025 political agreement and the July implementation date building exactly the workaround this measure doesn't reach: Temu signed a logistics agreement with DHL targeting 80% of its EU-sold products distributed from local warehouses by 2027; Shein expanded warehouse capacity in Wrocław, Poland, and is diversifying its supplier base away from China specifically to reduce duty exposure. Spain alone received 180 million such parcels in 2024, and roughly one in three Spanish online shoppers had bought from Temu or Shein in recent months — that customer base does not evaporate because the parcel-counting mechanism no longer catches goods arriving via consolidated bulk shipment into an EU warehouse instead of as individual low-value airmail parcels. Some real share of the Finnish drop may be substitution into a shipping model the duty doesn't touch, not genuine demand destruction — the Kaupan liitto web-traffic finding is the one piece of evidence here that points toward real behavioural change rather than pure supply-chain reclassification, and it is doing real work in this verdict leaning PARTIAL rather than lower.

Third, the enforcement-quality question Lange and Savvidis raised before implementation remains completely untested by this data. A fall in volume says nothing about whether the parcels that do still arrive are being correctly classified by category as the law requires, or whether under-resourced customs points are simply waving everything through at the flat minimum — which would understate revenue and quietly defeat the "fair competition" rationale even while the headline volume number looks like a win.

Cross-border texture worth noting: France had already run its own national small-parcel tax (2 € per category, introduced 1 March 2026) and suspended it on the exact day the EU measure took effect, specifically to avoid double taxation — a clean example of the fragmented national patchwork this measure was explicitly designed to replace, and of [escalating a decision upward only when there is a real reason to](/a-certain-vision/proximity-politics/). The EU returns 25% of collected duty revenue to the member state that processes the customs clearance, a fiscal detail worth tracking for whether it creates real enforcement incentive or just a modest revenue-sharing formality.

**The charge:** The EU set out to curb a demonstrated structural loophole and give European retailers a fair footing. The first hard data says the mechanism is working faster than skeptics predicted — but the same data cannot yet distinguish a genuine reduction in low-value import volume from a reclassification of the same commercial activity into a shipping model the duty doesn't reach, and [the enforcement-quality concern raised by the law's own architects](/a-certain-vision/earned-trust/) before implementation remains completely unaddressed by anything published so far.
