What Are You Working On?

JP Ahonen · 7 September 2026 · 9 min read

Everything written on this site so far has been a warning.

Borders used as weapons. Infrastructure held hostage. Alliances quietly emptied of their content while the language stays reassuring. All of it necessary, all of it true, and all of it the same shape: here is what threatens us, here is how it works, here is what it costs.

That is only half a politics. Nobody has ever built anything out of fear alone. And if you ask what Europe is actually for — not what it defends against, but what it is trying to become — the honest answer is that we stopped saying. Somewhere in the last forty years the language of European aspiration was replaced entirely by the language of European management. Competitiveness. Resilience. Coordination. Frameworks. Good words, all of them, and not one of them has ever made anyone want to get out of bed.

So this essay is the other half. It is not an analysis and it makes no attempt to be one. It is a description of a Europe that does not exist yet.

The Question We Ask

Walk into a public office in any European country today and the question underneath every form, every interview, every assessment is the same: what are you entitled to?

It sounds neutral. It isn't. It is a question about your deficits. To answer it you have to prove what you lack — that you are unemployed enough, poor enough, ill enough, desperate enough to qualify. You arrive as a claim to be assessed, and someone across a desk decides whether your account of your own life is credible.

Millions of Europeans do this every week. They arrive with what they can do, and they are asked instead to demonstrate what they cannot. Whatever they were building, learning, caring for, or trying to become is administratively invisible, because there is no box on the form where it goes.

I want a Europe that asks a different question.

What are you working on?

Not as pleasantry. As the actual organising question of the state's relationship to its citizens. Not what do you lack — what are you building? Not prove your need — tell us what you're for, and let us see what we can put behind it.

That is the whole idea. Everything else is mechanism, and mechanism is not what this essay is about.

What Changes When the Question Changes

Picture the same office, reorganised around the second question.

The person across the desk is not there to assess you. They are there to act for you. Part lawyer, navigating the bureaucracy on your behalf rather than making you navigate it. Part town crier, making sure the people who would benefit from what you're doing actually hear about it. Part patron, who knows which institution has the equipment you need and which professor takes an interest in exactly this, and picks up the phone. Part administrator, who fills in the form himself, because he does this every day and you do not.

They are not the judge of whether your work is worthwhile. Someone else, somewhere else, decides how scarce public resources get allocated, and the two roles never sit in the same chair — that separation matters enormously and it belongs in a different essay than this one. What matters here is that the person you actually meet is on your side. Their job is not to protect the state's resources from you. Their job is to connect the state's resources to you.

And what they mostly offer is not money. It is the key to a room that sits empty four nights a week. An introduction to someone who has spent thirty years on the problem you just discovered. The municipal newsletter, the local broadcast, the notice board that reaches the people you're trying to help. The knowledge that a piece of equipment you can't afford is sitting unused in a public laboratory eleven kilometres away, and that yes, you can be shown how to use it.

None of that is charity. Every one of those things already exists, already paid for, already sitting there. The only thing missing is anyone whose actual job is to connect it to the person who could use it.

Why I Believe This Works

I cannot prove any of what follows. That is worth saying plainly, because this site spends most of its time insisting on evidence, and I am about to stop.

What I have instead is something I have watched happen, repeatedly, in a smaller domain.

I spend a good part of my working life on the problem of athletes whose achievement is real and whose recognition is not — competitors at the top of sports the broadcast economics simply cannot sustain. They train the same hours. They sacrifice the same things. They win, and the win lands in silence, because no business model exists to point a camera at it. The talent was never the missing piece. The seeing was.

And what happens when they are finally seen is not what you would predict if you thought recognition was merely pleasant. It changes what they attempt next. It changes who finds them. It changes what they believe is available to them. Something that was already fully real, but invisible, becomes load-bearing the moment it is witnessed.

I think that generalises, and I think it generalises much further than sport.

I think there are people in every European town right now doing something real — caring for a neighbour, keeping a language alive, fixing something nobody was paid to fix, building something in a garage — that is invisible to every system we have, because our systems were designed to detect need and were never designed to detect contribution. And I think if we built the seeing, deliberately, at scale, with public resources behind it, we would find out that the productive capacity of this continent is dramatically larger than anything that currently shows up in our statistics.

Not because people would suddenly become better. Because they already are, and we have no instrument pointed at it.

The Part That Cannot Be Bought

There is an obvious wrong version of this, and it needs naming so it can be refused.

The wrong version turns recognition into currency. Points for good citizenship. A better rate on your mortgage because you volunteered. A scoring system, administered from somewhere central, that ranks your worth as a citizen and prices your access to ordinary life accordingly. That system exists in the world today and Europe should want no part of it.

The difference is not a matter of degree, it is a difference in kind. The moment contribution earns you a transactional advantage, it stops being contribution and becomes work — and badly-paid work at that, performed for a rate someone else sets. The instant you can buy it, you have destroyed the thing you were trying to encourage.

What I am describing pays nothing. It confers no advantage, unlocks no privilege, purchases no preferential treatment. It offers exactly one thing: that what you did is seen, named, and connected to the people it serves.

That has to be enough, or it isn't the thing.

What This Is Actually For

Somewhere in Europe tonight there is a nineteen-year-old who is not in work, not in education, and not in any statistic that describes him as anything other than a cost. He is quite good at something. Possibly very good. He has no idea, because nothing in his life has ever been organised to find out, and every institution he has met so far has approached him as a problem to be managed downward.

A Europe worth building is one where somebody's actual job is to find him and ask what he's working on. And then — this is the part that matters — to do something about the answer. Open the room. Make the introduction. Get him in front of the people who needed exactly what he can do and never knew he existed.

Multiply that by a continent.

I don't know what comes out the other side of that. Nobody does. That is precisely the point: we have never tried organising a society around the assumption that its people are a resource to be discovered rather than a liability to be administered, so nobody can honestly tell you what it would produce. My belief — and belief is the correct word, this is not a forecast — is that it produces things we cannot currently imagine, in medicine, in defence, in industry, in art, in a thousand places nobody thought to look.

Europe has spent forty years managing its own decline with great competence. We are extremely good at it now. We have the frameworks, the coordination mechanisms, the strategic reviews, the resilience agendas. What we have not had, in living memory, is a reason to get up in the morning that is larger than not losing.

I think the reason is available. I think it has been sitting in front of us the whole time, in the form of five hundred million people whose actual capacity nobody has ever bothered to look for.

We should go and ask them what they're working on.

JP Ahonen is a transformation director, Finnish defence reservist, and independent analyst based in Porto Rafti, Greece.
