Europe Is a Project, Not a Heritage Claim

JP Ahonen · 1 August 2026 · 7 min read

There are two ways to say the word Europe.

The first is a heritage claim. Europe as inheritance: cathedrals, borders, bloodlines, a museum with a security fence. In this telling, Europe already happened. The task of the living is custodial — guard the exhibits, restrict the entrance, resent the visitors. It is a comfortable story because it demands nothing except grievance.

The second is a project. Europe as a thing being built, badly and unevenly, by people who disagree about the blueprints. In this telling, Europe is not what we were given. It is what we are answerable for. The task of the living is construction — and construction is measurable, falsifiable, and frequently embarrassing.

This site is committed to the second telling.

The heritage trap

Heritage nationalism presents itself as strength. It is the opposite. A polity that defines itself by what it inherited has already conceded that it cannot define itself by what it does. It has retired from history and taken up curation.

The tell is always the same: heritage claims are unfalsifiable. No policy outcome can disprove them, no failure can embarrass them, no audit can touch them. "We are the heirs of Athens and Rome" survives any budget, any capability gap, any dependency on someone else's cloud, someone else's munitions, someone else's security guarantee. That is precisely why the claim is popular in capitals that have stopped delivering.

A project, by contrast, can fail. That is its virtue. If Europe is a project, then the question "is it working?" is always admissible — and the people running it can be held to account when the answer is no.

The evergray context

This matters now because the environment has changed. Europe no longer lives in peace punctuated by war. It lives in the evergray: permanent, managed hostility below the Article 5 threshold. Cut cables. Jammed navigation. Manufactured migration surges. Unattributable drones over airfields. Nothing declared, nothing concluded, everything deniable.

You cannot deter a hybrid campaign with a founding myth.

Heritage is useless here. The evergray rewards exactly the capacities that heritage nationalism neglects: infrastructure ownership, industrial depth, institutional speed, and the boring sovereignty of knowing who actually controls your systems when a subpoena arrives in a foreign jurisdiction.

The evergray also punishes the project's besetting vice — the announcement mistaken for the achievement. A "sovereignty package" that leaves every workload under foreign jurisdictional reach is not a defence; it is a press release with a flag on it. This site will keep a ledger of those gaps between the stated and the actual, because the gap is where Europe loses.

What this site does

CVPE — Certa Vizio Pri Eŭropo, "a certain vision of Europe," in a language invented by a Polish oculist precisely because no nation owned it — publishes three things.

Essays. Arguments about European sovereignty, security doctrine, institutional architecture, and civic identity. Where an essay makes a prediction, the prediction is logged with a timestamp and revisited in public, whether it aged well or not.

A lexicon. Precise words for things that are currently described vaguely, because vague description is how bad policy hides. When existing vocabulary fails, new terms are coined, dated, and defended.

An accusation audit. A standing record of where Europe falls short of its own stated values — not the shortfalls its adversaries invent, but the real ones, documented before someone else weaponises them.

The method throughout is what I have elsewhere called Ahonen's Razor: keep the problem, the attribution, and the solution separate. Most political failure begins by fusing them — choosing the villain first and reverse-engineering the diagnosis.

The civic wager

The project view of Europe rests on a wager: that identity can be built from participation rather than descent. A Finn in Greece, a Pole in Ireland, a Syrian in Sweden holding a fresh passport — the heritage claim has no coherent account of these people. The project does: they are co-builders, judged by contribution, bound by the same falsifiable standards as everyone else.

This is not sentimentality. It is the only definition of Europe that scales to the century we are actually in — one where the continent's adversaries are betting that heritage anxiety will do their work for them, fracturing the project from inside while the evergray grinds at it from outside.

Europe is a project. Projects have deadlines, budgets, owners, and post-mortems. Starting now, so does the commentary.
