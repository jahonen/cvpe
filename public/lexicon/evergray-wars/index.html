Evergray Wars

Coined JP Ahonen · 27 May 2026 · Bluesky exchange with Ulrike Franke, ECFR

Related: Proximity Politics · Sovereignty Washing

Evergray Wars (n.) — Permanent, managed hostility conducted below the Article 5 threshold: unmanned, hybrid, and infrastructural, never formally declared and never formally concluded.

The term names the condition Europe actually lives in, as opposed to the binary of war and peace its institutions were built for. Evergray conflict is not an interruption of normality; it is normality. Subsea cables are cut and repaired. Navigation signals are jammed and restored. Drones cross borders and are never claimed. Migration surges are manufactured at chosen pressure points. Each incident is individually deniable, individually survivable, and individually below any treaty trigger — which is the design.

The colour matters. Not the black of declared war, not the white of peace, and not the transient "grey zone" of the 2010s vocabulary, which implied a temporary ambiguity on the way to resolution. Evergray is permanent by intent. There is no settlement to negotiate because there is nothing declared to settle. The adversary's theory of victory is cumulative erosion: of infrastructure, of attention, of the credibility of institutions that keep announcing red lines the evergray is engineered to flow around.

Development

Coined 27 May 2026 in a Bluesky exchange with Ulrike Franke (ECFR) about whether "grey-zone conflict" still described the Baltic infrastructure campaign. The argument: "grey zone" frames the condition as an anomaly between two proper states. After three years of continuous subsea incidents, GNSS interference, and unattributed overflights, the anomaly framing was doing analytical harm — encouraging policymakers to wait for a "return to normal" that is not coming. "Evergray" was proposed as the correction: the permanence is the point.

Implications

Accepting the evergray framing changes the policy question from "how do we respond to this incident?" to "how do we run a society indefinitely under managed hostility?" — a question of infrastructure ownership, redundancy, industrial depth, and institutional speed rather than of retaliation. See Proximity Politics for the border-doctrine consequence, and Sovereignty Washing for the failure mode of answering an evergray problem with a branding exercise.
